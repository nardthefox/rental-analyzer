KPIs of a successful rental

These are the measures most investors check first. None of them is a guarantee. Treat them as a common language for “does this property pay its way?”

  • Monthly cash flow is what’s left after operating costs and the mortgage. A rental that pays you is easier to hold than one you have to feed.
  • Cash-on-cash return is that annual cash flow divided by the cash you actually put in (down payment, closing costs, fees, and any upfront rehab / CapEx). Many investors use something in the 8–12% range as a starting screen for a leveraged rental — your target may be different.
  • Cap rate is the property’s yield if you paid cash: yearly net operating income ÷ purchase price. It lets you compare two properties without mixing in a loan. Comfortable ranges depend on the market and the risk.
  • DSCR (debt service coverage) asks whether the rent covers the housing payment. Many DSCR lenders start around 1.00× (rent equals the payment); some want 1.20–1.25× for better terms. A cash purchase has no DSCR.
  • The 1% rule is a quick first filter: monthly rent around 1% of the price. It ignores taxes, repairs and the loan, and it’s often hard to hit in high-price markets. Use it to sort, not to decide.
  • Expense ratio is the share of rent that goes to vacancy and operating costs — not the mortgage. A common planning shortcut, often called the “50% rule,” is that about half of the rent may be eaten by those costs over time. Another published shortcut (the “55% rule”) plans for about 45% of rent to go to expenses, leaving 55% as net operating income.
  • Rules of thumb vary. A tight cash-flow market may reward appreciation more than yield. A lender’s minimum is not the same as your goal. Always check local rents, taxes and actual loan terms.
